How to read the Bot Checker results

The Bot Checker gives every Bluesky account a score and four category readings. This page explains what they mean, how they are calculated, and — just as importantly — what they cannot tell you.

The score

The score goes from 0 to 100. It shows how similar an account's behaviour is to a group of 598 Bluesky accounts that openly call themselves bots — compared with 581 ordinary accounts. The higher the score, the more the account looks like those declared bots.

The comparison group of bots consists mostly of scheduled feed accounts: accounts that automatically post news headlines, sports results, weather updates, and similar content on a fixed timetable. The score is best understood as a measure of similarity to this specific type of automation.

0 — looks like ordinary accounts100 — looks like declared bots

The seven result bands

The score is grouped into seven bands. Each band corresponds to how much the evidence tilts towards bots or ordinary accounts.

No sign of scheduled automation
The account's behaviour matches the ordinary accounts in the study on almost every measure.
Little sign of scheduled automation
The account mostly looks like the ordinary group, with a few measurements leaning bot-like.
Slightly more like ordinary accounts
A mixed picture that leans towards ordinary, but not by a wide margin.
Unclear
The measurements do not clearly separate this account from either group. Both interpretations are possible.
Some sign of automation
More measurements point towards the declared bots than the ordinary accounts, but the evidence is not decisive.
Strong sign of automation
The account looks very similar to the declared bots across most categories.
Very strong sign of automation
The account matches the declared bots in almost every measurement — the strongest possible signal.

Four categories of signals

The 37 individual measurements are grouped into four categories. Each category gets its own mini-scale so you can see at a glance which aspects of the account look bot-like and which look ordinary.

Profile

Basic facts about the account itself — how it was set up and what it looks like before you read any posts.

  • Number of followers and accounts followed
  • Whether it has a display name and a banner image
  • How many digits are in the handle
Timing

When and how often the account posts — the rhythm that separates a scheduler from a person typing in real time.

  • Posts per day since the account was created
  • How regular the gaps between posts are
  • Whether posts cluster at the same minute or second of the hour
Content

What the posts actually say — looking for the repetition and template-like structure common in automated feeds.

  • How similar posts are to each other (by words and characters)
  • Share of reposts, replies, links, and images
  • Post length, word variety, and near-duplicate posts
Social graph

Who follows the account — bot farms often attract other bots, and their follower base looks different from ordinary accounts.

  • Share of followers with empty or no-avatar profiles
  • Share of followers labelled as bots
  • How old the follower accounts are, and whether they cluster together

What this tool cannot tell you

The declared bots used to train the checker mostly post automatically on a schedule — news, alerts, weather. The tool is well suited to detecting that specific pattern. It is not a general-purpose fake-account detector.

A low score does not mean the account is a real person. A fake-person account with a natural posting rhythm, varied content, and genuine-looking followers can score very low. This is a known and important limitation of the approach.

A high score does not mean the account is a bot. Some ordinary accounts post on a strict schedule — for example, someone who shares one link every morning at the same time — and can look very similar to declared feed bots.

  • Use the reverse image search on the profile picture alongside the score.
  • Read a few posts and judge the content yourself.
  • Treat the score as one piece of evidence, not a final verdict.
  • Never use this result to publicly accuse a person of running a bot.
